New to Cyber Security

For students, career changers and anyone curious who wants a solid base before picking a specialty.

  1. Glossary: learn the core vocabulary first, starting with the CIA triad, threats, vulnerabilities and risk.
  2. Personal Security: secure your own accounts and devices before you defend anyone else's.
  3. Ports and Protocols: understand how computers talk to each other, since almost every attack crosses a network.
  4. Operating Systems: see where operating systems keep passwords, logs and settings, and why attackers want them.
  5. Windows 11: the most common desktop and a great first system to study in depth.
  6. Command Equivalents: compare Bash, PowerShell and CMD side by side so the command line stops feeling foreign.
  7. Bash basics: practice moving around and working with files on Linux.
  8. Learning platforms: pick a free lab platform and start doing hands on exercises.
  9. Certification Roadmap: choose a realistic first certification once you know what interests you.

Next step: the roadmap has a complete beginner path from Tech+ or A+ through Network+ to Security+.

SOC and Blue Team

For analysts, incident responders and anyone who watches alerts and investigates what happened.

  1. Glossary: get the defense terms straight, including SIEM, EDR, IOC and TTPs.
  2. Ports and Protocols: know what normal traffic looks like so the odd connection stands out.
  3. Wireshark Filters: dig into packet captures with ready made filters for common investigations.
  4. Windows 11 logs and artifacts: the event IDs and execution evidence used to build a timeline.
  5. Active Directory: where domain attacks happen and which logs show them.
  6. Ubuntu Server logs: the Linux side of the investigation, from auth logs to the journal.
  7. PowerShell triage: quick commands for live response on Windows hosts.
  8. DFIR and blue team sites: free tools, sandboxes and training for defenders.
  9. Threat intelligence: look up IPs, domains and hashes from your alerts.
  10. Utilities: decode Base64, check hashes and read JWTs without pasting evidence into random websites.
  11. My Cheat Sheet: star your favorite triage commands and keep them on one printable page.

Career track: see SOC Analyst Tier 1 on the roadmap, then move on to Tier 2, incident response or threat hunting.

Penetration Tester

For people who want to find and prove weaknesses through authorized testing.

  1. Ports and Protocols: every scan result is a port and a service, so learn what each one means and what to check.
  2. Kali Linux: set up and protect your own attack machine, including where tools keep their data.
  3. Bash networking: the command line skills you use on every engagement.
  4. Information gathering: start every test with reconnaissance and enumeration.
  5. Web application testing: the tools for the most common kind of target.
  6. Windows credentials and secrets: what to look for after you get a foothold.
  7. Active Directory: the heart of most internal tests, then the AD tools.
  8. Cloud Security: more and more targets live in AWS, Azure and Google Cloud.
  9. Utilities: encode payloads, decode tokens and calculate subnets offline.
  10. CTFs and wargames: practice legally until the techniques become second nature.
  11. Reporting: the report is what the client pays for, so learn to write it well.

Authorized use only: test only systems you own or have written permission to test. When you are ready, follow the Junior Penetration Tester track on the roadmap.

System Administrator

For admins and IT staff who keep servers and workstations running and want to keep them secure.

  1. Windows Server hardening: lock down domain controllers and member servers first.
  2. Ubuntu Server hardening: secure SSH, services and updates on Linux.
  3. Windows 11 hardening: bring every workstation up to the same baseline.
  4. PowerShell: automate Active Directory, Defender, BitLocker and updates across the fleet.
  5. Bash services and logs: manage systemd services and read the journal.
  6. CMD: the classic commands that still work on every Windows machine, including recovery.
  7. Ports and Protocols: close what you do not need and know the risks of what stays open.
  8. Cloud Security: IAM and logging basics for the cloud services you manage.
  9. Small team security: simple policies that protect the whole office, not only the servers.

Career track: the Security Administrator role on the roadmap is a natural next step from system administration.

Everyday User (Protect Yourself)

For anyone who wants to keep their accounts, money and family safe online, no technical background needed.

  1. The first six things: the few changes that block most attacks, done in order.
  2. Passwords: use a password manager and a unique password for every account.
  3. MFA and passkeys: add a second step to sign in, or skip passwords completely with passkeys.
  4. Phishing: spot fake emails, texts, calls and QR codes before you click.
  5. Devices and updates: keep phones and computers updated and locked.
  6. Mobile: safer settings for your phone and its apps.
  7. Backups and encryption: make sure a lost laptop or ransomware does not cost you your photos.
  8. If something goes wrong: what to do right away when an account is hacked or you clicked a bad link.
