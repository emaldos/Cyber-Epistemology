Awareness

Personal Cyber Security

Habits that stop most real attacks on people and small teams: unique passwords in a manager, passkeys or strong MFA, automatic updates, a pause before you click, and backups you have tested. Aligned with current NIST, CISA and NCSC guidance.

15 sectionsNIST SP 800-63B-4Reviewed November 2026

Start Here

If you only do six things, do these, in this order.

  1. Secure your main email account: it resets every other password. Give it a long unique password and a passkey or security key.
  2. Use a password manager: one unique, generated password per account. Reuse is what turns one breach into ten.
  3. Turn on passkeys or MFA everywhere it matters: email, bank, cloud storage, social media, work accounts and the password manager itself.
  4. Turn on automatic updates: operating system, browser, apps and router. Replace devices that no longer get security updates.
  5. Back up what you cannot lose: at least one copy offline or immutable, and test a restore.
  6. Pause on anything urgent: unexpected requests for codes, money, logins or remote access are scams until you verify them through a channel you already trust.

Passwords and Password Managers

Length and uniqueness beat complexity rules. Let a manager remember them.

Minimum length (NIST)15 characters when the password is the only factor, 8 when it is used with MFA. Sites should accept at least 64.
Composition rulesNIST SP 800-63B-4 says services shall not force mixes of uppercase, digits and symbols. Length matters more.
Periodic changesNot required. Change a password only when there is evidence it was exposed: a breach notice, phishing, malware or shared use.
BlocklistsServices should reject common, expected and already breached passwords, and allow paste, autofill and password managers.

Rules that work

  • One unique password per account. Never reuse a password, not even with small changes like a different number at the end.
  • Let the manager generate random passwords of 20 or more characters for sites you never type by hand.
  • For the few passwords you must remember (device login, password manager, main email), use a long passphrase of four or more random words, for example the NCSC three random words idea with an extra word.
  • No personal details: names, birthdays, pets, teams, addresses or anything visible on your social media.
  • Do not store passwords in plain notes, text files, chat messages, spreadsheets or screenshots.
  • Never share a password. Share access instead: shared vault folders, family plans or delegated access.
  • Security questions: treat them as extra passwords. Use random answers and save them in the manager.
  • Change a password right away if the service reports a breach, Have I Been Pwned lists it, or you typed it into a page you no longer trust.

Shared computers: never save passwords in a browser on a library, school, hotel or other shared computer, and always sign out when you leave.

MFA and Passkeys

A second factor stops most account takeovers. Phishing-resistant ones stop nearly all of them.

MethodPhishing resistantNotes
PasskeyYesBound to the real website, so a fake page cannot use it. Synced passkeys live in your password manager or Apple, Google or Microsoft account; device-bound passkeys stay on one device. Use them wherever they are offered.
FIDO2 security keyYesHardware key (USB, NFC) holding device-bound passkeys. Best choice for email, admin and finance accounts. Register two and keep the spare somewhere safe.
Authenticator app with number matchingPartlyMicrosoft Authenticator and similar. Number matching stops blind approvals, but a fake page can still relay the login in real time.
Authenticator app (TOTP codes)NoSix digit codes that change every 30 seconds. Much better than SMS, but a phishing page can ask for the code.
Push approval without number matchingNoVulnerable to MFA fatigue: attackers send prompts until someone taps Approve. Switch to number matching or a passkey.
SMS or voice codeNoBetter than nothing. Exposed to SIM swap, number porting and phishing. Use only when nothing else is offered.
  • Protect your password manager and main email with a passkey or security key first.
  • Register at least two methods (for example a passkey on your phone and a security key) so losing one device does not lock you out.
  • Save recovery codes offline: printed, or in the manager plus a paper copy for the manager itself.
  • Ask your mobile carrier for a SIM or port-out PIN to make SIM swap harder.
  • Review signed-in devices and app passwords in your main accounts and remove what you do not recognise.
  • A login prompt you did not start means someone has your password: deny it, then change that password.

Never read out or forward a code: no bank, support desk or delivery company needs your one-time code, and no one legitimate will ask you to approve a sign-in for them.

Devices and Updates

Supported systems, automatic updates and a few hardening switches.

Windows 11Current release is version 26H2 (September 2026). Home and Pro releases get 24 months of updates; 24H2 Home and Pro stopped on 13 October 2026, so move to 25H2 or 26H2.
Windows 10Support ended 14 October 2025. Consumer ESU on version 22H2 now runs to 12 October 2027 (free with settings sync, 1,000 Rewards points, or a one-time purchase). Plan the move to Windows 11 or a different system.
macOSApple fully patches only the latest major release. macOS 26 Tahoe is the last release for Intel Macs; macOS 27 needs Apple silicon.
Ubuntu and LinuxUse a supported release, for example Ubuntu 26.04 LTS (April 2026) or 24.04 LTS. Check end-of-life dates for your distribution.
Phones and tabletsStay on the newest major version your device supports. When the vendor stops security updates, replace the device.
Routers and IoTEnd-of-life routers and cameras are a common entry point. Replace them when firmware updates stop.

Hardening

  • Turn on automatic updates for the OS, browser, Microsoft Store or App Store apps, and use winget or your package manager for the rest.
  • Uninstall software you no longer use and anything that no longer gets updates.
  • Use a standard account day to day and a separate admin account for installs (least privilege).
  • Full disk encryption on: BitLocker or Device encryption on Windows, FileVault on macOS, LUKS on Linux. Store the recovery key somewhere other than the device.
  • Keep UEFI Secure Boot and TPM on, and set a firmware (BIOS or UEFI) admin password on laptops.
  • Windows: keep Microsoft Defender, Smart App Control (if available), Memory integrity and Windows Hello on.
  • Lock the screen when you walk away: Win+L on Windows, Ctrl+Cmd+Q on macOS. Auto-lock after 5 to 10 minutes idle.
  • Open untrusted files in a sandbox or VM first (Windows Sandbox on Pro and Enterprise, or a throwaway virtual machine).
  • Turn on Find My or Find My Device so a lost laptop or phone can be located, locked and wiped.

Email, Messages and Phishing

Scams arrive by email, text, chat, QR code and phone call. Urgency is the tell.

Stop, check, report

  1. Stop: urgency, secrecy, threats, prizes and unusual payment methods are pressure tactics.
  2. Check: verify through a channel you already trust (the official app, a saved number, walking over to a colleague). Look at the real sender address and the link target, not the display name.
  3. Report: use the Report phishing button in your mail app, tell IT at work, then delete the message.

Browsers and Extensions

The browser is where most attacks land. Keep it current and lean.

  • Use a current, auto-updating browser (Chrome, Edge, Firefox, Safari, Brave) and restart it when it asks.
  • Turn on HTTPS-only or always use secure connections, and Safe Browsing or SmartScreen protection.
  • Install few extensions, only from the official store, from publishers you trust. Remove ones you have not used in a month; extensions get sold and turned malicious.
  • Check extension permissions. "Read and change all your data on all websites" is a lot of trust.
  • Block third-party cookies and use a content blocker (uBlock Origin on Firefox, uBlock Origin Lite on Chrome and Edge) to cut malvertising.
  • Never allow notifications from sites you do not know. Fake "click Allow to continue" pages use them for scam pop-ups.
  • Use separate browser profiles for work, personal and risky browsing. Clear site data on close in the risky profile.
  • Search ads can lead to fake download pages. Download software from the vendor site you type yourself.
  • If privacy matters, check that your browser or VPN does not leak your IP address over WebRTC.

Public Wi-Fi

  • Most sites use HTTPS, so public Wi-Fi is less risky than it was, but prefer your phone hotspot for banking and work.
  • Never install anything a captive portal asks for, and ignore certificate warnings on public networks.
  • A VPN helps on untrusted networks. Use your employer's or a reputable paid provider; free unknown VPN apps often sell your data.
  • Turn off auto-join for open networks, and Wi-Fi and Bluetooth when you do not need them.

Small Team Basics

Extra controls for a small business, club or family office with shared accounts.

  • Require MFA for every account in Microsoft 365, Google Workspace and your other cloud tools, and use security keys or passkeys for admins.
  • Separate admin accounts from daily accounts, and keep at least two admins so one lost phone is not a lockout.
  • Use a business password manager with shared vaults instead of sharing passwords in chat or spreadsheets.
  • Write down onboarding and offboarding: when someone leaves, disable their accounts, rotate the shared passwords they knew and recover company devices the same day.
  • Set up SPF, DKIM and DMARC on your email domain so attackers cannot easily send mail as you.
  • Require a callback and a second approver for payments and bank detail changes.
  • Keep a short list of who to call for IT, the bank and insurance during an incident, printed and offline.
  • Check free schemes such as UK Cyber Essentials or the CISA Cyber Essentials guide as a baseline.

Mobile Devices

Your phone holds your passkeys, MFA and email. Lock it down like a laptop.

  • Use a six digit or longer passcode, plus Face ID, Touch ID or fingerprint unlock. Auto-lock after 30 seconds to 1 minute.
  • Install apps only from the official store, check the publisher and reviews, and avoid sideloading APK files or unknown profiles.
  • Review app permissions (location, microphone, camera, contacts, accessibility) and revoke what an app does not need.
  • Delete apps you do not use. Abandoned apps stop getting fixes.
  • Turn on theft protection: Stolen Device Protection on iPhone, Theft Detection Lock and Offline Device Lock on Android.
  • Turn on Find My (iPhone) or Find My Device (Android) and know how to erase the phone remotely.
  • Optional erase after 10 failed passcode attempts, if you have reliable backups.
  • High risk users (journalists, activists, executives): consider Lockdown Mode on iPhone or Advanced Protection on Android 16 and later.
  • Restart your phone once a week; it clears some memory-only malware.
  • Set a SIM PIN so a stolen SIM card cannot receive your texts in another phone.

Data, Backups and Encryption

Backups are your answer to ransomware, theft and mistakes, but only if they restore.

3 copiesThe original plus two backups.
2 mediaFor example the laptop disk and an external drive or NAS.
1 offsiteCloud backup or a drive kept at another location.
1 offline or immutableA drive you unplug after each backup, or cloud storage with versioning or object lock that ransomware cannot delete.
0 restore errorsTest a restore of real files regularly. An untested backup is a hope, not a backup.
  • Cloud sync (OneDrive, iCloud Drive, Google Drive, Dropbox) is not a backup on its own: deletions and encrypted files sync too. Keep version history on and add a separate backup.
  • Use the built-in tools: File History or Windows Backup on Windows, Time Machine on macOS, Deja Dup or rsync-based tools on Linux, iCloud or Google One backup on phones.
  • Encrypt backup drives and cloud backups, and store the key or password in your manager plus an offline copy.
  • Back up the things that are hard to replace: photos, documents, password manager export or database, MFA recovery codes, and any 2FA app backup.
  • Use end-to-end encrypted messengers (Signal, WhatsApp, iMessage) for private conversations, and turn on disappearing messages where it suits you.
  • For extra protection of iCloud data, turn on Advanced Data Protection where it is available in your country.
  • To send a sensitive file, encrypt it (for example a 7-Zip AES-256 archive) and send the password through a different channel.
  • Check file integrity with a SHA-256 hash when the publisher lists one (see Quick Commands).
  • Before selling or recycling a device, sign out of all accounts, remove it from Find My, and do a full factory reset with encryption on.

Privacy and Social Media

What you post is reconnaissance for scammers. Share less, and share later.

  • Set profiles to friends or private, and review who can find you by phone number or email.
  • Do not post routines, live locations, travel dates, boarding passes, tickets, ID documents, keys or car plates.
  • Avoid quizzes like "your first pet plus your street name": they collect security question answers.
  • Remove photo metadata (GPS location, device, date) before sharing files directly. Many platforms strip it, but email, cloud links and "send as file" usually do not.
  • Limit location history and app location access to "while using" or off.
  • Review connected apps and "Sign in with" grants in your Google, Apple, Microsoft and social accounts, and remove old ones.
  • Do not paste personal, customer or company confidential data into AI chatbots unless your organisation approves that tool.
  • Use a separate email address or aliases for shopping and newsletters to keep your main address out of breaches.
  • Delete accounts you no longer use instead of leaving them to be breached.
  • Consider opting out of people search and data broker sites, which feed targeted scams.

If You Clicked Something

A short incident plan. Speed matters more than perfection.

  1. Stop interacting: close the page or call. Do not enter more passwords, codes or card details, and deny any MFA prompts.
  2. Disconnect: turn off Wi-Fi or unplug the cable if you opened a file or installed something. Leave the device on if your IT team may want to investigate.
  3. Note what happened: time, sender, link or file name, what you typed and any screenshots. This helps IT, your bank and the police.
  4. Tell someone: at work, call IT or security right away; nobody gets in trouble for reporting fast. At home, call your bank first if money or card details are involved.
  5. Secure accounts from a clean device: change the password you entered and any account that used the same one, starting with email and the password manager. Sign out all sessions and check MFA methods, recovery email and mail forwarding rules.
  6. Scan and check the device: run a full antivirus scan, remove remote access tools, unknown apps, browser extensions, startup items and scheduled tasks.
  7. Protect your money and identity: freeze or replace cards, turn on transaction alerts, and consider a credit freeze if ID documents were shared.
  8. Rebuild if in doubt: for confirmed malware, back up only your documents, wipe and reinstall the system, then restore from a backup made before the incident.
  9. Report and learn: report the scam (see below), then fix what let it through: MFA, filters, a payment callback rule, or a conversation with the family.
If you typed a password into a fake page

Change that password now on the real site, and on every site where you used it. Sign out of all sessions, check for new MFA methods, app passwords, forwarding rules and recovery details the attacker may have added, and add a passkey so a stolen password alone is not enough next time.

If you gave a one-time code or approved a prompt

Assume the attacker is signed in. Change the password, use "sign out everywhere", remove unknown devices and authenticators, and check the account activity page. At work, report it immediately so IT can revoke sessions and tokens.

If you let a caller onto your computer

Disconnect from the internet, uninstall the remote access tool, and do not log in to banking on that device until it has been checked or reinstalled. Call your bank using the number on your card, and change important passwords from another device.

If you paid or sent money

Call your bank or card issuer at once; fast reports give the best chance of stopping or recalling a payment. For gift cards contact the card issuer, and for crypto report the receiving address to the exchange you used. Be wary of "recovery" services that contact you afterwards: they are usually a second scam.

If files are encrypted (ransomware)

Disconnect the device and any connected backup drives, do not pay, and contact IT or a professional. Check No More Ransom for a free decryptor, report the attack, and restore from an offline or immutable backup after the system is clean.
