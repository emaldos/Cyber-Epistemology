Overview

How transport protocols and port ranges work, and why a port number alone proves nothing.

TCPConnection oriented: a SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK handshake, ordered delivery and retransmission. Easy to scan reliably because closed ports answer with a reset.
UDPConnectionless, no handshake and no delivery guarantee. Scans are slow and unclear, and small requests with large replies make UDP services useful for reflection and amplification attacks.
Well known ports (0 to 1023)IANA system ports for core services. On Linux and macOS, binding below 1024 traditionally needs root or a capability such as CAP_NET_BIND_SERVICE.
Registered ports (1024 to 49151)IANA user ports assigned to applications on request. Many popular defaults in this range are vendor choices that were never registered.
Dynamic ports (49152 to 65535)Private or ephemeral ports for the client side of connections. Windows uses 49152 to 65535 by default, Linux uses 32768 to 60999 (net.ipv4.ip_local_port_range).
Protocols without portsICMP, GRE (IP protocol 47), ESP (50) and AH (51) sit beside TCP and UDP, so firewall rules for VPNs and tunnels must allow them by protocol number.

Tip: a port is a convention, not proof. Services run on any port and attackers favor 443, 53 and 80 because they are usually allowed out. Confirm the service with a banner grab or nmap -sV, and confirm the process on the host.

Remote Access and VPN

Shells, desktops and tunnels: the most attacked services on any internet edge.

Web and Proxies

Web servers, admin consoles and proxies. Most command and control traffic hides here too.

PortProtocolServiceNotes for defenders
80TCPHTTPCleartext. Redirect to HTTPS and send HSTS. Credentials or session cookies seen on 80 are already exposed.
443TCPUDPHTTPS, HTTP/3 over QUIC on UDPWhere most malware beacons. Inspect SNI, certificates and JA3 or JA4 fingerprints. Blocking UDP 443 makes browsers fall back to TCP, which proxies can inspect. SMB over QUIC also uses UDP 443.
8080TCPHTTP alternate (http-alt)Tomcat, Jenkins, proxies and admin consoles. Often forgotten, unpatched or left with default credentials.

1080TCPSOCKS proxyLegitimate in some networks, but also how attackers pivot (SSH -D, Chisel, C2 socks). Unexpected listeners on workstations are suspicious.
9090TCPPrometheus, Cockpit (vendor defaults)Metrics and server consoles that leak host details. Put them behind authentication or bind to localhost.
10000TCPWebmin (vendor default; IANA lists NDMP)Root level web admin with a history of remote code execution (CVE-2019-15107). Keep internal and patched.
7001TCPOracle WebLogic (vendor default)Frequent target of deserialization exploits. Never expose the admin console and apply Oracle quarterly patches.

Active Directory, Kerberos, RADIUS and name resolution fallbacks.

Data stores should only listen to application servers, never to the internet.

Well documented default ports of attack tools and old malware. Operators change them easily, so treat a match as a lead, not a verdict.

Warning: scan only networks you own or have written permission to test. Mature attackers use 443 and 53 rather than these defaults, so pair port rules with TLS fingerprints, DNS logs and process telemetry.

Exposure Checklist

Quick wins that remove most of the risk in the tables above.

  • Keep an inventory of internet facing ports and compare it with an external scan every month.
  • Default deny inbound and outbound at the edge; allow out only what users and servers need.
  • Never expose RDP, SMB, WinRM, databases, Docker, Kubernetes or OT protocols to the internet.
  • Replace Telnet, FTP, rsh, POP3, IMAP and SNMPv1 or v2c with SSH, SFTP, TLS variants and SNMPv3.
  • Disable LLMNR, NetBIOS over TCP/IP and SMBv1, and require SMB and LDAP signing.
  • Force DNS through internal resolvers and block unapproved DNS over TLS and DNS over HTTPS.
  • Block workstation to workstation traffic on 135, 445, 3389 and 5985 to slow lateral movement.
  • Patch VPN gateways, firewalls and remote access appliances first; they are the most exploited edge devices.
